A QUESTION IS NEVER A GROUNDING FAILURE. Questions make no factual claims.

GROUNDING is the criterion most prone to hallucinated rejections. Apply
this DEFAULT-PASS rule and the SUBSTANTIATION rule below before failing.

DEFAULT: PASS. The criterion is failed ONLY when you can substantiate the
failure by quoting a specific contradicting passage from the chunks.

YOU MAY ONLY FAIL on these two narrow conditions:

  (a) DIRECT CONTRADICTION — the draft states a specific fact that the
      chunks explicitly state differently. To fail, you MUST be able to
      quote the contradicting chunk passage verbatim in the revision_
      instruction (e.g. "Chunks say 'X', draft says 'Y' — these conflict").
      Phrasing differences are NOT contradictions — "tendons of nine
      muscles" and "nine flexor muscle tendons" describe the same fact.
      Word-order differences, synonym choices, and abbreviation are NOT
      contradictions.

  (b) INVENTED SPECIFIC PATIENT SCENARIO — the draft introduces a named
      occupation, age, medication, or personal detail with no anchor in
      the chunks (e.g. "your client is a 72-year-old dental hygienist on
      acetylcholine blockers"). Generic framing like "a patient with this
      injury" or "in clinical practice" is fine.

EVERYTHING ELSE PASSES, including:
  • Standard textbook anatomy facts a student would learn elsewhere
    ("the pinky and ring finger", "C8/T1 origin", "the medial epicondyle")
    — chunks do not need to repeat every fact you've ever read.
  • Anatomical inferences derivable from chunk content.
  • Generic clinical framing without invented details.
  • Different phrasing of the same fact.
  • Vocabulary not in the chunks but anatomically correct.

CRITICAL: if you cannot quote a contradicting chunk passage to substantiate
the failure, the criterion PASSES. Do NOT fail on a "feeling" that something
isn't grounded; only fail on demonstrable contradiction.
